# Next-Gen IT — Launch Plan
### From portfolio of assets to operating business
**Date:** August 2026 · **Principal:** Jeremiah Cargill · **Apprentice:** Myles

---

## The opportunity you're sitting on

You have every asset a boutique IT practice needs and none of the throughput. The domain audit portal exists, the runbooks exist, the brand exists, the competitive analysis of the title/escrow vendor space is done. What's been missing is motion — and Myles brought it to you unprompted. He was walking into businesses offering to build websites *before he knew how to build one.* That's not a sales resource; that's raw initiative looking for a craft to attach itself to.

The right move is not to hire a commission rep. It's to take someone with hunger and no ceiling and **build him into a practitioner.** He asked for a class. Give him an apprenticeship. The business gets throughput; he gets a trade. Both compound.

The model: **Myles learns by doing real work on real clients, under supervision, with responsibility that expands as competence does.** Websites are the training ground — cheap to deliver, forgiving of mistakes, obvious value to the client. The audit and care plan are where Next-Gen IT actually becomes a business, and where Myles eventually graduates to.

## Why apprenticeship beats a commission deal

A commissioned rep optimizes for the close and disappears after. An apprentice optimizes for getting better — which means he handles the awkward second revision, tests the site on a real phone, and calls the client back when the form breaks, because his name is on the work and not just the invoice.

For a practice whose entire differentiator is *"real architecture instead of another help desk,"* the person in the field has to actually know things. Myles is willing to learn them. That's rarer than sales ability and much harder to hire.

It also protects you. Every site he builds carries the Next-Gen IT name. Training him properly *is* the quality control.

## The apprentice ladder

Four stages. He advances by demonstrated competence, not time served. Tell him the whole ladder up front — eager learners move faster when they can see the next rung.

**Stage 1 — Shadow (weeks 1–2).** Completes crash course Sessions 1–2. Walks into businesses with the intake sheet; his only job is collecting complete intakes, not selling. Jeremiah builds. Myles watches every build and asks questions.
*Advances when:* 5 complete intakes collected, and he can explain DNS, hosting, and files without notes.

**Stage 2 — Build supervised (weeks 3–6).** Completes Session 3. Builds Starter sites himself on the crash course stack; Jeremiah reviews before anything goes live and owns all DNS and domain work.
*Advances when:* 2 sites shipped that passed QA on the first or second pass.

**Stage 3 — Own the delivery (months 2–4).** Runs Starter and Standard builds end to end, including client revision rounds. Jeremiah still holds domains, credentials, and final launch approval. Myles starts sitting in on audit reviews to learn the security side.
*Advances when:* a client renews into a care plan on a site he built.

**Stage 4 — Practitioner (month 5+).** Runs his own delivery queue, handles care-plan maintenance edits, begins learning the audit methodology. This is where the conversation about a real revenue split or a formal role happens — and by then it's a conversation about a proven person, not a bet.

## Earning while learning

He should make money from week one, structured to reward *getting better* rather than just closing.

| Stage | What he earns | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Shadow | $50 per complete, usable intake | Pays the hustle immediately; costs you nothing if the intake is junk |
| 2 — Supervised build | $200 flat per site shipped, plus the intake fee | Rewards finishing, not just starting |
| 3 — Own delivery | 35% of project fee on sites he sources and delivers | Real skin in the outcome |
| 4 — Practitioner | 50% on his sites, plus 10% of care-plan month one | Partner-track economics |

Two rules go in writing regardless of stage — a one-page agreement, not a handshake. **Client relationships, domains, and credentials belong to Next-Gen IT** (the *client* owns their domain; Next-Gen IT manages it; Myles never holds credentials personally). And **payment is on collected revenue only** — deposits before builds, always.

Frame the ladder to him straight: *"Stage 1 pays the least and teaches the most. You'll be through it in two weeks if you work."* Eager learners respond to that. They don't respond to being managed.

## The audit is the teaching tool and the sales weapon

When Myles brings back a business name, run the domain through the audit portal *before* the follow-up meeting — then walk him through what it found. He learns SPF, DKIM, and DMARC by seeing them broken on a real Georgetown business, not off a slide.

Then he walks back in with a scored 30-point report on their actual domain. That converts "we'll think about it" into "how much?" Nobody else knocking doors around here is doing that, and the reason he can is that he's being trained, not just deployed.

## 30-day launch sprint

**Week 1 — Arm and orient.** Crash course Session 1. Sign the one-page agreement. Print 25 intake sheets. Log the businesses Myles has already talked to in the pipeline tracker. He starts collecting intakes immediately — Stage 1 work begins day one.

**Week 2 — Volume at the door.** Crash course Session 2. Target 15 walk-ins, 5 complete intakes. Every intake gets a free domain snapshot within 24 hours, and Myles gets walked through each report's findings. This is the week he learns the most.

**Week 3 — First closes, first builds.** Crash course Session 3. Myles returns to snapshot businesses with the printed report and a quote. Target: 2 Starter deals with deposits collected. He builds at least one under supervision — Stage 2 begins.

**Week 4 — Ship and retro.** Both sites live on custom domains, both clients pitched the care plan at handoff. Sit down and review: what the intake sheet missed, which pitch lines worked, what broke in the build. Adjust the process — that retro *is* the curriculum.

**Success bar for month one:** 2 paid sites, 1 care plan signed, 15+ businesses in the pipeline, and Myles cleared through Stage 1. The last one matters as much as the revenue.

## Weekly operating cadence

One 30-minute call, same time weekly (Sunday evening fits around softball). Walk the pipeline top to bottom — every lead has a stage and a dated next action, or it gets marked dead. Myles reports doors knocked and intakes collected; Jeremiah reports builds shipped and snapshots run.

Then five minutes on one teaching topic he hit that week — a DNS record he didn't understand, a client objection he fumbled, a CSS thing that fought him. Small and consistent, and it's what keeps an eager learner engaged when the door-knocking gets repetitive. That five minutes is the retention strategy.

## What's deliberately out of scope

No paid ads, no cold email, no LinkedIn content engine, no new service inventions. One channel (Myles at the door), one wedge (websites), one upsell (care plan). The constraint is focus — and an apprentice can only learn one motion well at a time. Revisit at 90 days with real close-rate data.

## The real-estate lane runs in parallel

The Myles motion is main-street SMBs. Your existing real-estate/title targeting — the audit portal's original purpose — stays a Jeremiah-only lane. Those buyers need the security-posture pitch, not a $600 website, and the trust path runs through relationships rather than door-knocks. Don't blur the lanes: different buyers, price points, and closers. Myles graduates into that lane at Stage 4, not before.
