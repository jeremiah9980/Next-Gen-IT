Why this module existsrecurring > one-time

Module 1 taught you to ship a site. Module 2 taught you to find what's broken and sell the fix honestly. Both end with a check that clears once. Module 3 is where the company gets born: a maintained client is worth 3–5× a launched one over two years, and the maintenance only makes money if it takes minutes, not hours.

The trade you're making with the client is simple and you should say it out loud: "You pay a flat monthly rate. In exchange, your site stays up, backed up, and updated, your reviews keep coming in, and no phone call goes unanswered — and every month I send you a report showing exactly what happened."

1.2 — The proof-of-work report (the report IS the product)
40 min · build your template
▶

Clients don't cancel plans they can see working. They cancel invisible ones. The monthly report is five minutes of assembly and it is the single biggest driver of retention — treat it like the deliverable, because to the client, it is.

Subject: Your March report — Rosa's Bakery (all green) Hi Rosa, Here's what happened on your plan this month: ✓ Uptime: 100% — zero outages ✓ Backups: 4 of 4 ran clean (latest restore-tested Jan 12) ✓ Updates: 6 applied — no issues after ✓ Reviews: 3 new (you're at 47, avg 4.8★ — up from 44) ✓ Google profile: 2 posts published, 1,240 profile views (+9%) → Heads up: your domain renews April 30 — it's on auto-pay, nothing to do. One thing worth doing next month: your "wedding cakes" page gets the most views but has no photos from the last year. Send me 3 and I'll swap them in on your included edit time. — Myles · Next-Gen-IT · reply anytime

The rules: every line is verifiable, one "heads up" so they know you're watching, one suggestion so the plan feels alive, never longer than one screen. Numbers over adjectives.

1.3 — Scope walls & the change-order script
40 min · roleplay
▶

Care plans die from scope creep — "while you're in there, can you also…" Answer warmly, hold the wall. Included time is included; past that is a small quoted job. Clients respect walls that are explained before they hit them, so the walls go in the onboarding email, not the awkward moment.

The change-order script — warm, two sentences, a price"Happy to do that — it's about two hours of work, so it falls outside the plan's included time. I can do it for $150 flat and have it live by Friday; want me to?"
Inside the planOutside → quote it
Text/photo swaps within included minutesNew pages, redesigns, new features
Fixing anything the plan maintainsFixing things a third party broke (quote the rescue)
Questions, advice, "should I…" callsPrint design, logos, non-web projects
Never say "no" — say "that's a project"Every out-of-scope ask is a compliment and a sales lead. Log it. Three logged asks from one client is a Pro-tier upgrade conversation.
Session 2The five automations
~3 hrs · hands on keyboard
✓
2.1 — Missed-call text-back (the Pro-tier closer)
40 min · build + test
▶

A plumber under a sink can't answer. The caller dials the next plumber. 62% of calls to small businesses go unanswered at some point in the week — and an unanswered call is usually a lost job. Text-back turns a miss into a hold: within seconds the caller gets a text, and people who would never leave voicemail will answer a text.

Auto-reply on missed call: Hey — this is Dave's Plumbing. Sorry we missed you, we're on a job. Text us what you need + your address and we'll get back to you within the hour. If it's an emergency, reply 911JOB and we'll call you right back.
  • How it's built: a business line that supports auto-text on missed calls (OpenPhone and similar VoIP tools do this natively; a Zapier/Make hook works too). The client's existing number can usually forward in — never make them change their number.
  • The demo that closes it: at the pitch, call the client's phone, have them ignore it, watch the text land on their screen. Silence, then: "That customer just stayed yours."
  • The trap to avoid: if nobody answers the text either, the automation becomes an insult. Set the expectation honestly — the reply window promised in the text must be one the owner will actually keep.
✓
2.2 — The review engine on autopilot
35 min · you built this by hand in M2 — now automate it
▶

Module 2 taught the manual ask. On a care plan, the ask fires itself: job marked done → same-day text with the direct review link (the short g.page/r/… link, not "search for us on Google").

Thanks for choosing Dave's Plumbing today! If we did right by you, a quick review helps other neighbors find us: g.page/r/XXXX/review — takes about 60 seconds. Either way, thanks for the business. — Dave
The ethics lines — same as Module 2, now with a robot involvedAutomation makes it easier to do the wrong thing at scale, so recite it again: no review gating (you may not filter happy customers toward Google and unhappy ones away from it), no incentives for reviews, no fake reviews ever, and the ask goes to real customers after real jobs. An automated violation is still a violation — just a faster one.

Report line it feeds: "Reviews: 3 new this month, 47 total, 4.8★ average." That single line renews more plans than everything else combined.

✓
2.3 — Intake → one inbox
30 min · forms that never get lost
▶

Leads leak in three places: the form nobody checks, the inbox nobody labels, and the reply nobody sends. The fix is boring and beautiful:

  1. One destination. Site form → the owner's email and a spreadsheet row (timestamp, name, phone, message). The sheet is the memory; the email is the alert.
  2. Instant auto-reply to the customer: "Got your message — we reply within one business day. If it's urgent, call 555-0142." A lead that hears back in seconds waits; one that hears nothing shops on.
  3. A label and a rule. "Website Lead" label, auto-applied, so Monday's check is one click.

Test it like a customer: submit the form from your phone, time the auto-reply, confirm the sheet row. If any leg fails silently, the client finds out weeks later — from a lost job.

✓
2.4 — Backups, updates & the restore test
35 min · the unsexy core of every tier
▶

This is the part of the plan that's worthless right up until it's priceless. The standard:

  • Weekly backup, two locations. Host-side backup plus an offsite copy you control. One copy is zero copies.
  • Quarterly restore test. A backup you've never restored is a rumor. Restore to a staging copy, click around, log the date — that date goes in the report.
  • Updates on a schedule, not a whim. Batch them (Maintenance Monday, Module 5), snapshot first, check the site after. The snapshot-first habit is what separates "updated it" from "broke it at 5 pm Friday."
  • Uptime monitor pinging every 5 min with alerts to you, not the client. The report line: "Uptime 100% — zero outages."
Why the client can't DIY thisThey can — the same way anyone can change their own oil. They pay because it's reliably done, provably, without them thinking about it. That's the honest pitch, and it survives "prove it": the report proves it every month.
✓
2.5 — AI as apprentice, never as author of record
40 min · the leverage + the leash
▶

AI is how one person maintains twenty clients without drowning. It drafts; a human reads and sends. Where it earns its keep:

  • GBP posts: feed it the business, the month, one real photo's context → 3 post drafts in a minute. You pick one, fix one detail, publish.
  • Review replies: draft warm replies to every review — including the bad ones, where a calm drafted reply beats an owner typing angry at midnight.
  • Report assembly: paste your raw numbers, get the client-friendly email. You verify every number against the source before sending.
  • Service-page copy: first drafts from the owner's own words (record a 3-minute voice memo of them describing the job — that's your source material and it keeps the copy true).
The leash — recite it coldAI never sends anything unreviewed. AI never invents facts, prices, credentials, or dates. AI never writes reviews or testimonials — not "sample" ones, not "placeholder" ones, none. If a claim came from the model and not from the client, it doesn't ship.

3.1 — The upgrade pitch (two moments it works)
40 min · roleplay until smooth
▶

Moment 1 — at launch, while they're happy and the site is on the screen:

Launch-day attach"The site's yours — you own everything, like we agreed. From here you've got two options. Option one: I hand you the keys and you maintain it — updates, backups, the review asks — yourself. Totally fine. Option two: the care plan. $199 a month, and every month you get a report showing your backups ran, your updates applied, your new reviews, and your Google numbers. Most folks pick the plan because they didn't get into the bakery business to babysit software. Which way do you want to go?"

Moment 2 — the 30-day check-in, for the ones who said "I'll handle it":

Day-30 check-in"Quick check-in on the site — how's it going? When's the last backup you ran? … That's what usually happens, and it's exactly why the plan exists. No pressure — but if a month of maintaining it yourself was enough, I can take it off your plate starting today."

Both scripts share the DNA: real choice offered, self-serve path genuinely respected, plan described as a checklist with proof. Never fear-sell the plan ("hackers WILL get you") — Module 2 rules apply. The site being down for a weekend is scary enough when described accurately.

✓
3.2 — Baseline day: screenshot everything
30 min · the month-2 report writes itself
▶

The day a plan starts, capture the before: review count and average, GBP views/calls/direction-requests (last 30 days), site uptime status, current photo count on the profile. Save it in the client folder.

Month 2's report then opens with a delta — "Reviews: 44 → 47. Profile views: +9%." — instead of bare numbers. Deltas are what the client's spouse sees over their shoulder, and deltas are what renew plans. No baseline, no delta, no story.
