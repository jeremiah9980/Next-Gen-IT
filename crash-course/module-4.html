Session 1Territory & the list
~2 hrs · laptop + map
✓
1.1 — Build the 50
45 min · list before legs
▶

Knocking without a list is exercise, not sales. The list is 50 local, owner-operated service businesses where the Module 2 audit will find real problems. Sources, in order of yield:

  • The map crawl: search each trade ("plumber", "salon", "landscaping", "auto repair", "bakery") on Google Maps for your zone. Open each profile. No website link, a Facebook page as the "website", or a dead link → straight onto the list.
  • The drive-by log: every strip mall and trade van you pass. Van with a phone number and no web address is a lead painted on sheet metal.
  • New business filings & chamber lists: new businesses need everything and haven't bought it yet.

Tracker columns (the pipeline.csv from the launch kit): name · trade · address · phone · score · flags · stage · last touch · next step. A row with an empty "next step" is a dead row.

✓

30 min · rank the 50
▶

Work the list top-down by score. Points:

FlagPointsWhy it converts

Outdated site (5+ yrs old, wrong hours)+1Pride sale — slower, but sticky clients

✓
1.3 — Route the week: right door, right hour
30 min · 10 doors/day without wasted miles
▶

Ten doors a day, three field days a week, routed geographically so you're walking, not driving. And the hour matters more than the pitch:

TradeBest windowWhy
Contractors / trades7:00–8:00 amLoading the truck at the shop — after that they're gone all day
Restaurants / cafés2:00–4:00 pmBetween rushes; walk in at noon and you've lost them forever
Retail / salons10:00–11:00 amOpen, stocked, pre-lunch lull, owner often on-site
Auto shopsMid-afternoonMorning drop-offs done, bays working, counter's free
The person matters more than the hourIf the owner isn't there, you haven't knocked — you've visited. Get the owner's name and their usual hours from whoever is there, log it, and come back. Never pitch staff; it burns the door.
Session 2The conversation
~3 hrs · scripts, then roleplay until boring
✓
2.1 — The 20-second opener + the leave-behind
40 min · time yourself
▶

You get twenty seconds before an owner decides you're a solicitor or a person. The opener earns the next two minutes, nothing more — its job is a conversation, not a sale.

The opener — local, specific, evidence in hand"Hi — are you the owner? I'm Myles, I'm with Next-Gen-IT, we're local. I'm not here to pitch you for an hour — I looked up how your business shows up online before I walked in, and I found a couple things I'd want to know about if it were my shop. Takes two minutes to show you. Got two minutes, or should I leave it with you?"
  • "Are you the owner?" first — never waste the opener on staff.
  • "I looked you up before I walked in" — instantly separates you from every flyer-dropper they've ever waved off.
  • The exit ramp ("or should I leave it with you?") lowers the pressure, which raises the yes rate. People lean toward doors that aren't being pushed.

The leave-behind is the Module 2 findings page — one page, their business name on it, ≤6 findings, severity-ordered, your number at the bottom. Not a brochure. Brochures are about you; the findings page is about them, which is why it survives the counter for weeks.

✓
2.2 — Discovery: five questions, 70/30 listening
40 min · the sale happens here, quietly
▶

Once they give you the two minutes, resist the urge to present. Ask, and shut up. They should talk 70% of the time — every answer is either a qualification, an objection defused early, or the exact words you'll use in the close.

  1. "How do most of your customers find you right now?" — baseline; "word of mouth" means Google is untapped upside.
  2. "What happens when someone calls and you're mid-job?" — tees up missed-call text-back (M3) without naming it.
  3. "Who handles the email and the website stuff now?" — finds the nephew before the nephew finds you.
  4. "Have you ever paid anyone for this before? How'd that go?" — surfaces scar tissue; you'll inherit their last guy's sins unless you ask.
  5. "What would one more good job a week actually be worth to you?" — they price the value themselves. Their number is always bigger than your quote.
Write down their wordsThe close is their own sentences played back: "You said a bathroom remodel runs about four grand and you're missing calls every week — the whole setup is $1,500 once and $199 a month. It pays for itself the first time it catches one call."
✓
2.3 — Quoting: three tiers, one breath, then silence
40 min · say the number like it's the weather
▶
PackageOne-timeWhat it isCare plan attach
Starter$750One sharp page: who, what, where, hours, click-to-call, review link, done in a weekEssentials $99/mo
Standard$1,5005 pages · services broken out · photo gallery · intake form · GBP built or rescuedStandard $199/mo
Pro$3,000Everything above + booking/scheduling or light e-commerce · content written from owner interviewsPro $299/mo

Present all three, recommend one: "Most shops your size take Standard — here's why." Three options make the middle feel sensible; one option makes any price feel like a demand.

The silence ruleSay the price, then stop talking. Count five in your head if you have to. The person who speaks first after the number is negotiating against themselves — and it will not be you, because you practiced this exact silence in roleplay until it stopped feeling rude.
✓
2.4 — The five objections, drilled cold
60 min · open each one, say it out loud
▶

Objections aren't attacks; they're the owner thinking out loud. Every answer below shares a spine: agree with the true part, add the missing fact, hand back a small decision. Tap each to open:

1"That's too expensive."
"Compared to what it costs today, yeah, it's real money. Here's the math I'd run: you said a job's worth about $400. The Standard setup is $1,500 once — four jobs, ever, and it's paid for. After that every job it brings in is profit. And if the audit's wrong about what you're missing, don't buy anything. Want me to leave the findings page so you can check my work?"
2"My nephew handles our website."
"That's honestly great — family that shows up is worth keeping. Two questions I'd want answered either way: when's the last backup he ran, and who has the passwords if he's busy in finals week? If he's got both covered, you're in better shape than most shops on this street. If he's not sure — I'm not here to replace him, I'm here so it's somebody's job instead of somebody's favor."
3"I don't have time for this right now."
"That's exactly the pitch, honestly — the whole point is it takes zero of your time. I need about 20 minutes of you total: one sit-down for photos and your story, and one look before it goes live. Everything else is my problem. When's your slow day — I'll come to you."
4"Leave your card, I'll call you."
"Sure thing — and look, nobody ever calls, and that's fine, you're busy. Tell you what: I'll leave the findings page, not a card. It's got the three things I found with your name on them. I'm back on this street Thursday — if it made sense, two minutes then. If not, keep the page, fix them yourself, no hard feelings." (Then actually come back Thursday. The comeback IS the close.)
5"All my business is word of mouth — I don't need internet customers."
"Word of mouth is the best marketing there is — I'm not going to argue with what's working. One thing though: word of mouth moved. When your customer tells their neighbor about you, the neighbor Googles you before calling. Right now that Google search shows [the actual finding: nothing / the wrong hours / a competitor with 80 reviews]. I'm not selling you new customers — I'm making sure the referred ones actually land."
Session 3Paper, pipeline, and pay
~2 hrs · the boring parts that make it a business
✓
3.1 — The one-page agreement
30 min · walk it line by line
▶

One page, plain English, signed at the counter. Handshakes are for meeting people, not scoping projects. The six blocks:

  1. Scope — exactly what's being built, by package name and page count. What's listed is included; what isn't, isn't.
  2. Price & deposit — total, 50% today, 50% at launch. Deposit starts the clock.
  3. Timeline — a real date, with the client's dependency named ("photos and approvals within 5 days, or the date slides").
  4. Ownership — the Module 2 promise on paper: domain, hosting, and content belong to the client. Full stop. This clause wins deals against every agency that holds sites hostage.
  5. Care plan opt-in — a checkbox with the tier and monthly price, cancelable anytime with 30 days' notice. Cancelable-anytime closes more plans than any pitch; locked contracts create ex-clients.
  6. Signatures + date.
✓
3.2 — Deposit first, always. Then never chase.
30 min · money rules
▶
  • No deposit, no work. Not a keystroke. A client who won't pay half up front was never a client — they were a quote collector, and you just found out for free.
  • Invoice on the spot. Payment link sent from your phone while you're still standing there. "Want to knock out the deposit now so I can get you on this week's build schedule?" Every hour between yes and invoice is where deals evaporate.
  • The pause rule: final payment overdue 7 days → friendly nudge; 14 days → work and plan pause, stated kindly and in advance (it's in the agreement). You never have to be angry, because the paper does the enforcing.
The day-14 pause, said warmly"Hey — wanted to flag that the launch invoice is a couple weeks out now, so per our agreement I've paused things until it's settled. Soon as it's in, everything goes right back live same day. All good otherwise?"
✓
3.3 — Pipeline discipline + the comp plan, cold
45 min · the weekly rhythm
▶

Five stages, no more: List → Contacted → Discovery → Quoted → Closed/Lost. Every row moves or gets a reason. The rhythm:

  • Weekly pipeline call with Jeremiah — numbers first (doors, convos, quotes, closes), stories second.
  • The two-minute loss debrief on every "no": which stage did it die in, what was the last thing they said, would a different hour/opener/price have mattered? Written in the tracker while it's fresh. Losses debriefed become wins; losses shrugged off become patterns.
The comp plan — recite it exactly, because money deserves precisionOn collected revenue only: 50% of site fees on deals you sourced AND built · 20% on deals you sourced that Jeremiah built · 10% ongoing spiff on care-plan revenue from your clients, for as long as they stay. Nothing is owed on quotes, handshakes, or invoices — only on money in the account. Around deal #10 we put the rep→LLC conversion conversation on paper.

Why "collected only" is said out loud on day one: it aligns everyone toward clients who actually pay, and it means a chargeback or a deadbeat never turns into an awkward clawback conversation between us.
